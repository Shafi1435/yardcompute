Driveway Gravel Calculator Guide

If you are asking how much gravel you need for a driveway, start with the driveway area and planned gravel depth. Then convert the volume to cubic yards and, when your supplier sells by weight, estimate tons using the material's bulk density.

Quick answer: how much driveway gravel do I need?

For a rectangular driveway:

Volume = length × width × depth

When length and width are in feet and depth is in inches:

Cubic feet = length × width × (depth ÷ 12)

Then add your planning allowance, convert cubic feet to cubic yards by dividing by 27, and use supplier density to estimate tons.

For example, a 40 ft × 12 ft driveway with 4 inches of gravel is about 160 cubic feet, or 5.93 cubic yards, before an allowance. With 10% added, it is about 176 cubic feet, or 6.52 cubic yards. At 105 lb/cu ft, that is about 9.24 tons.

How to calculate gravel for a driveway

1. Measure the driveway

Measure the driveway length and average width. If the shape is irregular, divide it into simple rectangles or other manageable sections and calculate each section separately.

2. Choose the gravel depth

Use the depth specified for your project. Driveway construction can involve multiple aggregate layers, so calculate each layer separately when depths or materials differ.

3. Calculate cubic feet

If length and width are in feet and depth is in inches, divide the depth by 12 before multiplying.

40 × 12 × (4 ÷ 12) = 160 cubic feet

4. Add a planning allowance

An allowance can help account for compaction, spreading, measurement variation, and waste. If you use 10%:

160 × 1.10 = 176 cubic feet

5. Convert to cubic yards

Gravel is commonly ordered by cubic yards or tons. Convert cubic feet to cubic yards by dividing by 27:

176 ÷ 27 = 6.52 cubic yards

6. Estimate tons

When the supplier sells gravel by weight, use the material's published bulk density:

Tons = cubic feet × density ÷ 2,000

At 105 lb/cu ft:

176 × 105 ÷ 2,000 = 9.24 tons

Density varies with material type, gradation, moisture, and supplier. Use supplier-provided density whenever available rather than assuming one universal value.

7. Estimate truckloads

If you know the truck's usable payload, divide estimated tons by that payload and round up:

Truckloads = ceiling(estimated tons ÷ truck payload)

For 9.24 tons and a 10-ton payload, the estimate is 1 truckload. Actual delivery limits can depend on the supplier, truck, access, and local rules, so confirm the payload with the supplier.

Should driveway gravel be calculated in layers?

Yes when the driveway uses different aggregate layers. Calculate each layer from its own depth and material density, then combine the quantities. Do not assume the same density or depth for every layer.

Common driveway gravel calculation mistakes

  • Mixing inches and feet without converting depth.
  • Using a generic density when the supplier provides a different value.
  • Ignoring irregular driveway sections or edges.
  • Using total driveway depth for every individual layer.
  • Ordering exactly the mathematical minimum without considering compaction, spreading, or waste.
  • Assuming a truck can carry any calculated quantity without checking its actual payload.

FAQ

How much gravel do I need for a driveway?

Calculate driveway length × width × gravel depth to get volume. Convert inches to feet when needed, add a practical allowance, then convert to cubic yards or tons using supplier information.

How many cubic yards of gravel do I need for a driveway?

Calculate the driveway volume in cubic feet, include your planning allowance, then divide by 27 to convert cubic feet to cubic yards.

How do I convert driveway gravel to tons?

Multiply the adjusted cubic-foot volume by the gravel density in pounds per cubic foot and divide by 2,000.

How deep should driveway gravel be?

There is no single depth that fits every driveway. The appropriate depth depends on the existing base, soil, drainage, traffic, aggregate, and project design. Follow the specified depth for your project.

Why does gravel density matter?

Different aggregate materials and conditions can have different bulk densities. The same volume can therefore have different weights, which affects a ton-based order.

Should I add extra gravel?

A planning allowance can account for compaction, spreading, waste, and measurement variation. Verify the final quantity with your supplier before ordering.

How many truckloads will I need?

Divide the estimated tons by the truck's usable payload and round up. Confirm the actual payload and delivery limits with the supplier.
