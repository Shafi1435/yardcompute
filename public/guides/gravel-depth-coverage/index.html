How to Calculate Gravel Depth and Coverage

Gravel quantity depends on the area you need to cover and the depth of the gravel layer. The basic calculation is simple, but mixing inches and feet or using an inconsistent depth can produce a wrong order quantity.

1. Measure the area

For a rectangular area, measure the length and width in feet. Multiply them to get square feet.

Area = length × width

For example, a 20 ft × 10 ft area has 200 square feet.

2. Choose the gravel depth

Use the depth specified for your project. Measure the planned layer thickness rather than guessing from the amount of material you intend to buy.

If depth is given in inches, convert it to feet by dividing by 12. For example, 3 inches is 0.25 ft and 4 inches is about 0.333 ft.

3. Calculate gravel volume

Multiply the area by the depth in feet:

Volume = area × depth

Example: 200 sq ft at 3 inches deep:

200 × (3 ÷ 12) = 50 cubic feet

4. Add a planning allowance

Depending on the project, an allowance can account for minor measurement differences, spreading, settlement, compaction, and waste. If you use a 10% planning allowance, multiply the base volume by 1.10.

50 × 1.10 = 55 cubic feet

The appropriate allowance varies by project, so treat it as a planning input rather than a universal rule.

5. Convert volume when buying by weight

Suppliers may sell gravel by tons rather than cubic feet. To estimate weight, use the bulk density supplied for the material.

Tons = cubic feet × density in lb/cu ft ÷ 2,000

For example, 55 cubic feet at 105 lb/cu ft is about 2.89 tons. Actual density varies with material type, grading, moisture, and supplier.

Irregular areas

For an L-shaped, curved, or otherwise irregular area, divide the project into simple sections that can be measured separately. Calculate each section and add the volumes together. Keep the planned depth consistent unless different areas intentionally use different depths.

Why depth changes the quantity so much

Area tells you how much surface is covered, while depth determines how much material is stacked over that surface. Doubling the depth doubles the calculated volume when the area stays the same. That is why a small depth error can create a meaningful difference in the material estimate.

Common mistakes

  • Using inches as though they were feet.
  • Using one depth for areas that actually have different layer thicknesses.
  • Confusing square feet with cubic feet.
  • Assuming every gravel material has the same density.
  • Treating a planning allowance as a guaranteed waste percentage.

FAQ

How do I convert gravel depth from inches to feet?

Divide the depth in inches by 12. For example, 4 inches equals 0.3333 feet.

How much gravel is needed for a 200 square foot area?

The amount depends on depth. At 3 inches, the base volume is 50 cubic feet before any planning allowance.

Does deeper gravel always mean more material?

Yes. With the same area, volume increases in direct proportion to depth.

Should I use the same gravel density for every project?

No. Density varies by aggregate type and supplier. Use supplier information when available.

How should I calculate an irregular area?

Break it into measurable sections, calculate each section separately, then add the volumes.
