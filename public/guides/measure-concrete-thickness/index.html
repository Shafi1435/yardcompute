How to Measure Concrete Thickness

Concrete thickness is easy to guess and hard to see. You usually need it for one of two reasons: you want to know what an existing patio, walkway, or driveway slab is made of (before you overlay it, cut it, anchor into it, or pour next to it), or you are about to pour a new slab and want to be sure the forms are the depth you plan to order for. This guide covers both.

Quick answer

For an existing slab, the most reliable home method is to dig along one edge and measure from the top surface to the bottom of the concrete, then confirm with a small test hole away from the edge, because many slabs are thicker at the edges than in the middle. For a new slab, stretch a string across the top of the forms and measure down to the compacted base at several points. Take at least three readings and use the average for ordering.

Measuring an existing slab: four methods

1. Measure at an exposed edge

Where the slab meets soil or a planting bed, dig a small hole right against the edge until you can see or feel the bottom of the concrete. Hold a straightedge flat on the slab surface, reaching out over the hole, and measure straight down from the straightedge to the bottom of the concrete. Repeat on two or three sides.

Keep in mind that edges are often thickened on purpose (a "turned-down" edge), and soil may have washed out from under them. An edge reading tells you the edge thickness, which may be more than the field of the slab.

2. Drill a small test hole

To check the middle of the slab, drill a test hole with a hammer drill and a masonry bit in an out-of-the-way spot. Wrap tape around the bit as a depth flag, drill until the bit suddenly drops or brings up soil or gravel instead of concrete dust, then measure the hole depth with a thin rod, a wire bent into a hook, or the depth rod on a tape measure. Hook the bottom edge of the concrete with the bent wire for a more exact reading. Fill the hole afterward with concrete patch or anchoring epoxy.

Before you drill: make sure there is nothing in the slab you could hit. Indoor and garage slabs can contain radiant heat tubing, electrical conduit, or plumbing. Some slabs are post-tensioned, with steel cables under high tension; these are often marked with a stamp or sign, and they should never be drilled or cut without a professional scan. Wear eye and hearing protection, and control dust: drilling concrete releases crystalline silica, so drill with water or a vacuum attachment and wear a properly fitted respirator.

3. Use an opening that already exists

A floor drain, a cracked or broken corner, an old post hole, a pipe penetration, or a section that was saw-cut out can show the full thickness without drilling. Measure the cut face from top to bottom. Check that you are measuring the slab itself and not a thicker footing below it.

4. Check records or hire a scan

Permit drawings, the original contractor's quote, or the builder's plans may list the specified thickness. A concrete scanning or coring company can measure thickness without guessing: ground-penetrating radar (GPR) or ultrasonic testing is non-destructive, and a core drill removes a cylinder you can measure directly. This is worth paying for before cutting or coring a slab that may contain cables, conduit, or tubing.

Measuring a new slab before you pour

For a new slab, the thickness is set by the gap between the top of the forms and the compacted base, so measure that gap, not the form boards alone.

  1. Compact the gravel base and set the forms to their final height and slope.
  2. Stretch a tight string line across the tops of opposite forms.
  3. Measure straight down from the string to the base at several points across the slab, including the middle and near each corner. A simple grid every few feet catches high and low spots.
  4. Rake high spots down or add and compact base in low spots, then measure again.
  5. Use the average of your readings in the calculator, and plan for the thinnest point being at least your minimum design thickness.

Lumber is smaller than its name. A 2x4 form board is about 3.5 in tall and a 2x6 is about 5.5 in, so a slab formed with 2x4s on a flat base is about 3.5 in thick, not 4 in. If you want a full 4 in, the base must sit lower than the bottom of the boards.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring only at the edge and assuming the whole slab is that thick.
  • Reading the nominal lumber size (2x4) as the slab thickness.
  • Taking one reading on an uneven base instead of averaging several.
  • Measuring from the top of a gravel base that has not been compacted yet; it will settle and the slab will end up thicker than planned.
  • Drilling or cutting a slab without checking for tubing, conduit, or post-tension cables.

FAQ

How can I tell how thick my concrete patio is without drilling?

Dig along an edge and measure the exposed face, or look for an existing opening such as a drain, broken corner, or old post hole. Remember the edge may be thicker than the middle. For a non-destructive reading of the middle, a scanning company can use ground-penetrating radar or ultrasonic testing.

How thick is a typical concrete patio or driveway?

Patios and walkways are commonly poured about 4 in thick, and driveways are commonly thicker because they carry vehicles. The right thickness depends on loads, soil, climate, and local rules, so check your local requirements before you plan a new slab, and measure an existing one rather than assuming.

What size drill bit should I use for a test hole?

A small masonry bit in a hammer drill is enough; the hole only needs to be wide enough for a wire or thin rod to reach the bottom. Choose a bit long enough to pass through the slab you expect, plus a little extra.

Should I use the thinnest or the average reading?

Use the average to calculate how much concrete to order. Use the thinnest reading to judge whether the slab meets the minimum thickness your project needs.

Does a thicker edge change the concrete order?

Yes. Calculate a thickened edge as a separate strip (its length × width × extra depth) and add it to the slab volume. The Concrete Footing Calculator can help with continuous strips.
